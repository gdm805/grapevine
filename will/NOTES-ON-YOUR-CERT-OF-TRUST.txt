NOTES ON YOUR CERTIFICATION OF TRUST
=====================================

WHAT THIS IS
A Certification of Trust proves a trust exists and shows the Trustee's authority, without handing
over the whole trust document (which usually says who gets what -- private information a bank
doesn't need). Almost every state has a statute that lets a Trustee use one instead of the full
trust when opening an account or retitling property.

WHY THIS ONE SHARED DOCUMENT, NOT 51 SEPARATE ONES (like your DPOA, HCD, and HIPAA authorization)
You sent a national baseline plus 51 of your own state-specific Certification of Trust files. I
read all 51. They are NOT the same document with a name swapped in, the way your DPOA and HCD
files are -- each state's file phrases things differently, splits sections differently, and even
uses different token names for the same underlying fact. Converting all 51 as fully separate
documents would have been a much bigger build, on the order of the DPOA build or larger.

What the 51 files DO have in common: every one of them is certifying the same handful of core
facts -- practically all of them trace back to the Uniform Trust Code's own model certification
provision (UTC section 1013), which is why "the same fact, worded differently" is the pattern
across states rather than genuinely different requirements. And every state only requires a
notary -- I checked all 51, and not one requires witnesses for this particular document.

So this document uses one shared body -- covering the facts every state's statute actually asks
for (trust name and date, who created the trust, the current Trustee(s) and their authority,
whether the trust is revocable, the Trustee's powers, and how title is typically taken) -- with
your state's own statutory citation and your state's own notary acknowledgment wording spliced in
at the end, taken word for word from your controlled Cert_of_Trust_<State>.docx files. This is the
same approach used for your revocable living trusts (single and joint): one shared body, a
per-state execution page.

WHAT'S SIMPLIFIED COMPARED TO YOUR SOURCE FILES
Your source files include several features this version doesn't ask about, because they're
uncommon or add complexity most people creating their own trust online won't need:
  - Transaction-specific certifications (naming a particular pending sale or transfer and the
    specific powers needed for it). This version is a general-purpose certification instead.
  - A trust director / power of direction (a separate person who directs the Trustee on certain
    decisions). Uncommon outside of larger or more customized trusts.
  - The more granular Trustee-action rules some states offer (action authority that varies by
    transaction type, or a fully custom rule quoted from the trust). This version offers the three
    common rules: any one Trustee may act, all must act together, or a majority may act.
  - Per-Trustee "capacity" labels (original Trustee vs. successor Trustee, etc.) and a description
    of how a successor Trustee came into office. Every currently acting Trustee is just listed as
    "Trustee."
  - The power to revoke a revocable trust is automatically the Trustmaker(s) themselves, matching
    how Grapevine's own trust builders work, instead of asking a separate question.
Say the word if you'd like any of these added as an option.

THE NOTARY BLOCK: SAME CONVENTION AS YOUR OTHER DOCUMENTS
Every state's notary acknowledgment blanks (county, date, the signer's name as the notary will
verify it) are left blank for the notary to fill in by hand at the signing appointment -- a notary
won't accept a pre-filled acknowledgment anyway. The one thing that IS filled in automatically is
the trust's name, since that's already known and isn't something the notary verifies. Louisiana's
block keeps its own civil-law wording (a notarial "Extract of Trust," a Parish instead of a
county) rather than being forced into the same shape as the other 50 states.

STATUTORY CITATIONS
Every state's citation came from your own controlled file, except a handful where your file didn't
state one explicitly (California, Kansas, Kentucky, Louisiana, Michigan, South Dakota, Vermont,
Washington, West Virginia) -- for those, I looked up and filled in the state's actual
certification-of-trust statute. New York and Rhode Island genuinely have no dedicated
certification-of-trust statute; their citations say so and point to the general trust code instead.
As with every other document on this site, these are unverified drafts -- have an attorney confirm
the citation and the requirements before this is used for anything real.

WHAT THE QUESTIONS COVER
Your trust's name and date (plus whether it's been restated or amended), the Trustmaker(s), the
current Trustee(s) and their addresses, how multiple Trustees act together (if there's more than
one), whether the trust is revocable, any limitation on the Trustee's authority (optional), and how
title is typically taken in the trust's name.

HOW TO EDIT
File: will/cert-template.js     the Certification document itself (settings + the full text)
File: will/cert-state-text.js   the state-by-state statutory citation and notary block
File: will/cert-signing.js      the signing steps shown after review
The notes at the top of each file explain the codes used ([[if x]], [[each trustmakers]], and so on).
